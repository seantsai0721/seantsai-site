<Section n="01" label="Site" title="Taiwan's largest oyster coast is losing its lagoon.">

Dongshi produces nearly half of Taiwan's oysters, raised on racks in the shallow lagoon behind the Waisanding sandbar. Cut off from its sediment supply, the sandbar keeps shrinking and is projected to disappear by 2028. When it goes, the lagoon and most of the town's livelihood go with it.

On land, decades of groundwater pumping for fishponds caused subsidence and salinization. Farmers are abandoning their ponds, and the low ground now floods at least once or twice every summer.

<Section n="02" label="Question" title="Same species, a twentieth of the price.">

Taiwanese lagoon oysters sell for NT$50–100 per kilogram. Raw-grade oysters from France sell for NT$1,500–2,000. The difference is the farming environment: high-value oysters grow in controlled seawater ponds and pass through finishing and depuration before sale. Lagoon racks offer no such control, and stock dies when conditions change.

The project asks whether Dongshi's abandoned fishponds could become that controlled environment, and whether the same ground could also protect the villages from flooding.

</Section>

<Section n="03" label="Strategy" title="Two linked systems on the abandoned ponds.">

Usable ponds are converted into a land-based, raw-grade oyster farm. Ponds that have already turned to marsh, about 60 hectares, become a wetland that cleans the farm's nutrient-rich effluent and holds stormwater. Treated water returns to the farm, so the two systems feed each other.

<Section n="04" label="Mechanism" title="A landscape that switches with the season.">

Dongshi has a distinct dry and wet season, and the wetland changes role with it. From December to March, when farm effluent peaks during oyster finishing, it works as a staged filter. From May to September, when typhoons and southwesterly storms arrive, it drains down and becomes a sponge that absorbs runoff from the villages and the coastal expressway.

<Section n="05" label="Architecture" title="A pavilion shaped like the shell it serves.">

The depuration hall takes its layered roof from the growth rings of an oyster shell. The folds carry solar panels and channel the summer southwesterly wind through bladeless turbines, so the sterile nursery runs on its own power.
