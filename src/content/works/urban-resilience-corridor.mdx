<Section n="01" label="Site" title="A marsh that once joined a mountain to a river.">

As Taipei grew, the ecosystems around it were cut into fragments, shrunk or lost. Fewer habitats mean less biodiversity, and without resilient natural ground the city has less capacity to absorb extreme weather.

The site sits between the Keelung River and Jiantan Mountain, the western end of the Wuzhi Mountain range. A 1930 map shows it as marsh linking the two. After a Shinto shrine was built nearby, the water shrank and new roads began to cut the ecosystems apart. From 1953 the land was covered with buildings that split the two sides completely. It later became a park, but one planned without habitat, rarely used and never improved.

<Figure src="/works/urban-resilience-corridor/history.webp" alt="Four maps and aerial photos of the site in 1930, 1945, 1953 and 1973 to today, showing the marsh disappearing under roads and buildings" caption="1930 · 1945 · 1953 · 1973 to today" />

Bei'an Road beside the park floods 0.3 to 1 metre deep when rain reaches 100 mm an hour.

</Section>

<Section n="02" label="Concept" title="Let the two ecosystems meet again.">

The design follows how the landscape changed and rebuilds the link between the mountain and the river. Reshaped ground creates varied habitats and a corridor for wildlife, stores stormwater against extreme rain and screens the park from the noise of heavy traffic. The project has three aims: resilient space, habitat restoration and public wellbeing.

<Figure src="/works/urban-resilience-corridor/concept.webp" alt="Concept diagram: arrows from the Wuzhi Mountain ecosystem and the Keelung River ecosystem meet at a restored habitat in the park, which also collects surface runoff from the road" caption="Mountain and river ecosystems meeting in a restored habitat" />

</Section>

<Section n="03" label="A · Passage" title="A wildlife passage under the road.">

A 40-metre ecological passage runs beneath Bei'an Road and through the flood wall, reconnecting the hill ecosystem with the riverbank for mammals, reptiles and amphibians. On the river side, the concrete embankment is replaced with a rock revetment of sandstone boulders 500 to 1,000 mm across, at least a quarter set below the water. The gaps between the stones shelter fish, shrimp and crabs, and aquatic planting gives them places to feed and spawn.

<Figure variant="wide" src="/works/urban-resilience-corridor/section-a.webp" alt="Section A–A′ through the wildlife passage under the road, with a rock revetment, aquatic plants, herons and fish at the river edge" caption="Section A–A′, 1:100. Animals not to scale." />

</Section>

<Section n="04" label="B · Habitat" title="Land, shore and water at different depths.">

Changes in level create a range of conditions, from dry ground through waterside wetland to shallow and deep water, so wildlife can live, feed and breed here. The aim is to rebuild the mix of species found where a low-mountain ecosystem meets a river.

</Section>

<Section n="06" label="F · Flood" title="A basin that takes the road's floodwater.">

The sunken landform holds about 1,900 cubic metres of stormwater. An open kerb along Bei'an Road, with a 15 cm gap between the boardwalk and the road, lets runoff flow straight into the basin, which fixes the flooding on this stretch of road. When the basin is full, the overflow drains to the Keelung River through the wildlife passage. The design gathers and slows rainwater instead of only draining it, to prepare for more extreme weather.

Improved soil helps trees grow into a healthy urban forest. Planting favours native, high-carbon species such as camphor, Taiwan acacia and Griffith's ash, together absorbing about 1,790 kg of carbon dioxide a year.

</Section>
