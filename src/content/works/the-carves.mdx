<Section n="01" label="Site" title="An island that became a peninsula, then a landfill, then a museum.">

Before 1936, Badouzi was an island separated from Keelung by a narrow channel. In 1937 a power plant filled the channel for its cooling water, and the island became a peninsula. From 1975 a landfill on its east coast piled waste about 15 metres high and almost buried a local landmark hill, until residents forced it to close in 1992. The power plant became the National Museum of Marine Science and Technology in 1997, and restoration parks have been built over the landfill since 2000.

The coast has good scenery, geology and ecology, but it sits between two national scenic areas and gets little of their attention. Its native ecology is intertidal and grassland: the northeast monsoon is too strong for trees on exposed ground.

<Section n="02" label="Strategy" title="Use the force that shaped each place.">

Every part of the east coast has been carved, by waves cutting the rock platform or by people filling and dumping. The plan links three zones and borrows the largest outside resource each one has.

<Section n="03" label="Trace" title="A harbour that faces the visitors again.">

Paths are regraded so residents keep their working routes while visitors split off toward the harbour. The entrance plaza beside the museum's historic building is opened up, restoring the power plant's old water intake and reconnecting the museum to the sea and the village.

The breakwater bridge is reshaped so it no longer blocks the view to the Jiufen hills. After testing different angles and curves, a 60° concave edge deflects the wind upward and leaves a calm strip for people crossing. Shops and village experiences gather along the harbour front, while the homes behind keep their privacy.

</Section>

<Section n="04" label="Tide" title="Letting the sea back onto the rock.">

Visitors to the museum stay almost entirely indoors. Here, fill is removed to expose the original wave-cut platform and seawater is brought in to form a man-made intertidal zone. The remaining walls keep it safe for school groups, and the sea's own movement keeps maintenance low. Parking moves half underground, out of the view and out of the salt wind.

Wall heights follow the local tide record: mean sea level 7 cm, mean high water 39 cm (highest 78 cm), mean low water −40 cm (lowest −88 cm). The top is broken into heights between 0 and +50 cm, some at +100 cm, so parts stay above water even at high tide.

</Section>

<Section n="05" label="Gaze" title="Shelter from the monsoon, aimed at the view.">

The headland looks out to Heping Island, Keelung Islet, Keelung Mountain and Jiufen, but the sea wind kills almost everything planted here except ground cover, and wave erosion has undermined the old paths and stairs.

The earth-covered buildings take their layered profile from the exposed strata of nearby Qidou Mountain, and their lines point visitors toward the buried landmark hill. The wind walls borrow the curve of the native Taiwan lily. Their height and direction follow the views: the east wall turns toward Keelung Mountain at 45°, the south wall steers the eye to the Qidou cliffs. Of three profiles tested, a concave wall worked best, turning the northeast wind aside to leave sheltered pockets where coastal shrubs and lilies can take hold.
